W-T2-04 — Problem / Solution: Traffic Congestion in Large Cities

Writing Task 2 · Causes + measures · 288 words · 40 minutes · original prompt, not from any past paper

Prompt. Traffic congestion in large cities is getting worse. What are the causes, and what measures can governments take? Give reasons for your answer and include any relevant examples from your own knowledge or experience. Write at least 250 words.

1. Sample answer (band 8+)

Commuters in many large cities now spend hours each day stationary in traffic. The main drivers are rising car ownership and poorly connected public transport, and governments can respond with better transit investment combined with charges that discourage unnecessary driving. This essay will explain these causes and then outline two measures that directly address them.

Congestion has grown partly because incomes have risen while cars have become cheaper, putting a second vehicle within reach of many households. At the same time, public alternatives remain unattractive: buses share the same jammed roads, suburban rail coverage is patchy, and last-mile connections are weak. As a result, even residents who would prefer the train continue to drive. Urban sprawl reinforces the pattern, since new housing estates are built far from workplaces with few shops or schools nearby, forcing long commutes. Each family therefore adds several car trips per day to roads that were planned for far lower volumes.

Governments can break this cycle by pairing investment with restraint. The first step is dedicated public transport that bypasses traffic, such as bus lanes, metro extensions and reliable feeder services, which gives drivers a genuinely faster alternative. Singapore shows how park-and-ride hubs linked to frequent trains pull commuters out of cars. The second step is pricing: congestion charges and higher central parking fees make short or solo car journeys reflect their true cost, with the revenue ring-fenced for transit upgrades. Introduced together, these measures match each cause — poor alternatives and cheap driving — rather than simply widening roads, which quickly fills again.

In short, traffic worsens because driving is easy and alternatives are weak. Where states fund credible public transport and price road use honestly, congestion can be slowed and eventually reversed.

2. Why it scores

  • TR: Band 8 — both questions answered fully and solutions are matched to the stated causes ("these measures match each cause — poor alternatives and cheap driving"). Ideas are extended with mechanisms, not listed; a generic solution paragraph would leave parts unanswered and cap TR.
  • CC: Band 8 — cause paragraph chains linked steps ("As a result", "reinforces the pattern", "therefore") and the solution paragraph sequences two steps explicitly. Cohesion comes from idea logic; a memorised linker chain would cap CC at 6–7.
  • LR: Band 8 — precise transport range: "last-mile connections", "park-and-ride hubs", "congestion charges", "ring-fenced for transit upgrades". Each term fits this prompt exactly; forcing them into other topics would cap LR as collocation error.
  • GRA: Band 8 — relative clauses carrying specification ("roads that were planned for far lower volumes", "transport that bypasses traffic") plus participle results ("forcing long commutes"). Wide range, majority error-free.

4. Grammar frames that carry ideas

  • Frame: Since + planning cause, + forced effect — example: "since new housing estates are built far from workplaces…, forcing long commutes". Job: ties urban form to behaviour with a participle result.
  • Frame: The first step is X…, the second step is Y — example: "The first step is dedicated public transport… The second step is pricing". Job: sequences matched solutions so each maps back to a cause.

5. Outline to copy (not sentences)

  1. Intro: paraphrase problem → name two causes + two matched measures → map
  2. Body 1 (causes): affordability + weak alternatives → sprawl mechanism → volume result
  3. Body 2 (measures): transit investment + city example → pricing + revenue use → cause-match check
  4. Conclusion: restate cause pair → conditional result (if funded + priced, congestion reversible)

6. 10-minute task + do not copy

Task: Write a two-sentence cause→matched-solution pair for a new problem (e.g. plastic waste): cause with a "forcing/forces" result, then a solution that names the same cause back. 10 minutes.
Do not copy: "Singapore shows how park-and-ride hubs linked to frequent trains pull commuters out of cars" — works only here because the prompt is about urban congestion; reusing Singapore for unrelated topics would cap TR for unsupported drift.
