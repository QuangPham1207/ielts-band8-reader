W-T2-05 — Two-Part: Why Young People Leave Villages

Writing Task 2 · Two-part (reasons + evaluation) · 292 words · 40 minutes · original prompt, not from any past paper

Prompt. Many young people leave rural areas to live in cities. Why does this happen, and is it a positive or negative trend? Give reasons for your answer and include any relevant examples from your own knowledge or experience. Write at least 250 words.

1. Sample answer (band 8+)

Across many countries, villages are losing school-leavers to regional capitals and megacities. The shift is driven by better-paid work and wider services in urban areas, and while it brings real gains for migrants, I consider the overall trend negative because of what it strips from rural communities. This essay will explain the economic and social reasons for the move before assessing its effects.

Young people leave mainly for employment and education. Rural economies often depend on seasonal farming or a single industry, offering low wages and few graduate posts, whereas cities concentrate factories, offices and universities. A nursing graduate, for instance, may find only part-time shifts locally but several full-time hospital posts with training in the city. Social life matters too: cinemas, sports clubs and broader friendship networks make urban living feel freer, especially where village norms are restrictive. Once a few peers relocate and send back wages or stories, migration chains form and departure starts to look like the normal path.

The trend benefits individuals but damages both ends of the migration. Cities gain workers yet face overcrowded housing, strained schools and informal settlements when arrivals outpace planning. Meanwhile villages lose their youngest and most skilled residents, leaving ageing populations with fewer carers, teachers and entrepreneurs. Farmland may be abandoned and local clinics closed for lack of staff, which in turn pushes more families to leave. Although remittances and new skills partly compensate, they rarely rebuild a hollowed-out community. For that reason, the personal opportunities are real, but the collective outcome is difficult to call positive.

In sum, rural youth move for jobs, education and urban freedoms, and the move often improves their prospects. Nevertheless, because it overloads cities and depletes the countryside, the pattern is on balance a negative one.

2. Why it scores

  • TR: Band 8 — answers both parts with no drift: paragraph 2 explains reasons, paragraph 3 evaluates them, and the verdict ("on balance a negative one") directly answers "positive or negative". Each part is extended with examples and results.
  • CC: Band 8 — the two questions are sequenced by paragraph role, with internal chains ("Once a few peers relocate…", "which in turn pushes more families to leave"). Contrast is earned ("Although remittances… partly compensate, they rarely rebuild"); template linkers would cap CC at 6–7.
  • LR: Band 8 — precise migration range: "seasonal farming", "migration chains", "informal settlements", "hollowed-out community". "Hollowed-out" is a skilful uncommon item used naturally; deploying it outside depopulation contexts would cap LR.
  • GRA: Band 8 — concessive control throughout ("while it brings real gains", "Although remittances… partly compensate", "where village norms are restrictive") plus participle causes ("leaving ageing populations"). Wide range, majority error-free.

4. Grammar frames that carry ideas

  • Frame: Once + chain trigger, + normalisation result — example: "Once a few peers relocate and send back wages or stories, migration chains form and departure starts to look like the normal path". Job: turns one example into a self-reinforcing social mechanism.
  • Frame: Although + partial compensation, + denial of full repair — example: "Although remittances and new skills partly compensate, they rarely rebuild a hollowed-out community". Job: concedes the other side's best point, then limits it to protect the verdict.

5. Outline to copy (not sentences)

  1. Intro: paraphrase shift → preview verdict (gains for migrants, net negative) → map (reasons, then assessment)
  2. Body 1 (why): jobs + education gap → concrete graduate example → social freedoms → chain effect
  3. Body 2 (evaluation): city costs + village losses → feedback loop → concede remittances → verdict (collective negative)
  4. Conclusion: mirror intro (reasons + personal gain) → restate net judgement

6. 10-minute task + do not copy

Task: Reuse the "Although X partly compensates, it rarely…" concession frame for a new evaluation (e.g. tourism income vs heritage damage): concede one real gain, then limit it in the same sentence. 10 minutes.
Do not copy: "A nursing graduate, for instance, may find only part-time shifts locally but several full-time hospital posts with training in the city" — works only here because the prompt is about rural-to-urban youth migration; pasting the nurse elsewhere would cap TR as off-topic support.
