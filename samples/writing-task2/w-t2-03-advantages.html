W-T2-03 — Advantages Outweigh: Tourism in Historic Cities

Writing Task 2 · Advantages outweigh disadvantages · 275 words · 40 minutes · original prompt, not from any past paper

Prompt. Tourism brings major benefits to historic cities, but it also causes serious problems. Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages? Give reasons for your answer and include any relevant examples from your own knowledge or experience. Write at least 250 words.

1. Sample answer (band 8+)

Historic cities from Venice to Kyoto now receive visitor numbers their streets were never designed to carry. Although overcrowding brings undeniable harm, I believe the economic and cultural gains of tourism still outweigh the damage, provided visitor numbers are managed. This essay will weigh the income and preservation benefits against congestion and housing pressure before giving a final judgement.

The strongest argument for tourism is the revenue that funds preservation itself. Entrance fees, hotel taxes and spending in local shops give municipalities the budget to restore temples, maintain old quarters and employ conservation specialists. Without this income, many historic buildings would decay because public funds alone cannot cover the cost. In Prague, for example, ticket income from major sites has financed the repair of facades and flood defences that residents also enjoy. Tourism also sustains craft workshops and traditional performances that would otherwise lose their audience, keeping heritage alive rather than frozen behind glass.

The main costs are congestion and pressure on housing. Crowded lanes, litter and rising rents push long-term residents out of central districts, turning living neighbourhoods into short-stay zones. Daily life becomes harder when transport is full and local shops give way to souvenir stalls. Yet these problems stem less from visitors themselves than from unmanaged volume. Caps on cruise ships, timed entry to popular sites and licensing limits on holiday rentals have already eased pressure in several European cities. Where such controls exist, residents keep their streets while still receiving tourist income.

Overall, the damage is real but controllable, whereas the funding and cultural vitality tourism provides are difficult to replace. Cities should therefore limit numbers rather than discourage visitors altogether.

2. Why it scores

  • TR: Band 8 — answers the outweigh question directly with a decisive verdict ("still outweigh the damage, provided visitor numbers are managed") and weighs both sides rather than listing them; the final judgement matches the introduction.
  • CC: Band 8 — the rebuttal turn is handled by paragraph-internal logic ("Yet these problems stem less from… than from unmanaged volume") instead of stacked linkers; each paragraph keeps one central topic. Memorised chains of stacked over-used linkers would cap CC at 6–7 as mechanical cohesion.
  • LR: Band 8 — topic-precise range: "restore temples", "conservation specialists", "short-stay zones", "timed entry". Items collocate with heritage management rather than generic "tourism vocabulary" deployed regardless of context, which would cap LR.
  • GRA: Band 8 — concessive and conditional control ("Although overcrowding…", "Without this income…", "Where such controls exist…") with a majority of error-free sentences and flexible subordination.

4. Grammar frames that carry ideas

  • Frame: Without + income/source, + consequence — example: "Without this income, many historic buildings would decay because public funds alone cannot cover the cost". Job: proves necessity by imagining the funding removed.
  • Frame: Yet + problem + stem less from X than from Y — example: "Yet these problems stem less from visitors themselves than from unmanaged volume". Job: concedes harm while relocating its cause, opening the door to solutions.

5. Outline to copy (not sentences)

  1. Intro: paraphrase tension → verdict (benefits outweigh, conditional on management) → map
  2. Body 1: main benefit (preservation revenue) → funding mechanism → city example → cultural spillover
  3. Body 2: main harm (congestion + housing) → lived effect → reframe cause (volume, not visitors) → proven controls
  4. Conclusion: restate verdict (harm controllable, gains irreplaceable) → policy line (limit, not ban)

6. 10-minute task + do not copy

Task: Apply the "stem less from X than from Y" reframe to a new outweigh topic (e.g. social media harms): concede two harms, relocate the cause to unmanaged use, then name one control. 10 minutes, no full copying.
Do not copy: "In Prague, for example, ticket income from major sites has financed the repair of facades and flood defences" — works only here because the prompt is about historic cities; moving Prague into another essay would cap TR as irrelevant support.
