S-P3-01 — TEACHERS & LEARNING

Speaking Part 3 · Discussion · 4 questions · 3–5 sentences per answer · original prompt, not from any past paper

Paired Part 2 topic. Describe a teacher who had a positive influence on you. You should say who the teacher was, what they taught you, how they taught, and explain why this teacher was memorable. Part 3 extension. The discussion below moves from that one teacher to teaching and learning in general: what makes teachers effective, classroom versus self-study, technology, and lifelong learning.
Questions. 1. What qualities make a good teacher? 2. Is classroom learning better than studying on your own? 3. How has technology changed the role of teachers? 4. Why is lifelong learning becoming more common?

1. Sample answers (band 8+)

Q1. What qualities make a good teacher?
I think the most important quality is the ability to explain difficult ideas in simple, concrete terms. Subject knowledge matters, of course, but plenty of experts cannot communicate what they know. A teacher who notices when students are confused and adjusts the explanation tends to get far better results. I saw this with my own physics teacher, who would switch to everyday examples whenever the textbook definition failed.

Q2. Is classroom learning better than studying on your own?
It depends largely on the learner and the subject. Classrooms provide structure, deadlines and immediate feedback, which many students need to stay on track. Self-study, on the other hand, allows faster learners to move at their own pace and focus on their weak points. For practical subjects like languages, though, I would say interaction with others is hard to replace entirely.

Q3. How has technology changed the role of teachers?
Technology has probably shifted teachers from being the main source of information to being guides who help students use it. Since facts are instantly available online, the teacher's job is increasingly about selecting reliable material and teaching critical thinking. In my country, for example, many schools now use short video lessons for theory and keep class time for discussion. That said, the change is uneven, and schools with limited resources may not have felt it much at all.

Q4. Why is lifelong learning becoming more common?
The main driver is likely that jobs change faster than they used to, so qualifications go out of date more quickly. People in fields like healthcare or software often need to retrain every few years just to stay employable. There is also a personal side: online courses have made it cheaper and easier for adults to study in the evenings. Even so, it remains difficult for people working long hours, so it is not equally accessible to everyone.

2. Why it scores

  • TR (addresses all parts, extends with ideas): each answer takes a direct position and develops it, e.g. Q2 answers "It depends largely on the learner and the subject" then weighs both sides instead of listing. Band logic: fully relevant, ideas extended with explanation or example. A memorised opener such as "This is a highly debatable question" would add nothing to the position and cap TR, since it answers no part of the question.
  • CC (logical flow, natural reference): progression is carried by qualifying phrases and paragraph logic rather than stacked linkers — "That said, the change is uneven" concedes and limits the claim in Q3. Band logic: flexible cohesion within each answer, no mechanical chains. Copying a "Moreover / Furthermore / In conclusion" template would cap CC at 6–7 for mechanical cohesion.
  • LR (precise collocation, natural hedging): topic-specific but unforced items — "stay on track", "stay employable", "go out of date", "critical thinking". Hedging ("tends to", "is likely", "may not have") avoids over-generalisations, which is part of band 8 precision. Dropping in a pre-learned "fancy" phrase regardless of context would cap LR for inaccurate collocation.
  • GRA (range with control): mixes relative clauses ("who notices when students are confused"), conditionals ("whenever the textbook definition failed"), and concessive structures ("Even so, it remains difficult…"). Errors, if any, are slips rather than breakdowns. Memorised complex frames forced onto new content typically break agreement or tense — those errors count against GRA.

4. Grammar frames that carry ideas

  • Frame: "It depends largely on X" + two-sided comparison — example: "It depends largely on the learner and the subject." Job: gives a balanced answer without sitting on the fence; works for any "which is better" question.
  • Frame: concession limiter, "Even so / That said, + limitation" — example: "Even so, it remains difficult for people working long hours". Job: qualifies your own argument, showing critical thinking; reusable whenever you generalise.

5. Outline to copy (not sentences)

  1. Q1: state top quality → why knowledge alone is insufficient → classroom example.
  2. Q2: qualify ("depends") → strength of option A → strength of option B → verdict for one case.
  3. Q3: name the shift → explain with contrast (before/now) → concrete example → limit the claim.
  4. Q4: main economic reason → personal/accessibility reason → concession about who is excluded.

6. 10-minute task + do not copy

Task: Retell the Q3 answer aloud in your own words in under 45 seconds, keeping the "shift → example → limit" shape but changing the subject to doctors or drivers. Record and check you hedged at least twice.
Do not copy: "I saw this with my own physics teacher, who would switch to everyday examples whenever the textbook definition failed." — works only here because it answers a question about explaining; transplanting it caps LR (irrelevant detail) and TR (unanswered question).
