S-P3-02 — CITIES & QUIET SPACES

Speaking Part 3 · Discussion · 4 questions · 3–5 sentences per answer · original prompt, not from any past paper

Paired Part 2 topic. Describe a quiet place where you like to spend time. You should say where it is, what it is like, when you go there, and explain why you enjoy it. Part 3 extension. The discussion below widens from that personal place to cities in general: why urban areas are noisy, how noise affects people, whether governments should protect quiet areas, and what cities may look like in future.
Questions. 1. Why are cities noisier than the countryside? 2. How does noise affect people's lives? 3. Should governments do more to protect quiet spaces in cities? 4. How might cities change in the future to become more peaceful?

1. Sample answers (band 8+)

Q1. Why are cities noisier than the countryside?
The obvious reason is density: far more people, vehicles and construction are packed into a small area. Commercial life adds another layer, from late-night restaurants to delivery traffic early in the morning. Countryside areas have some of these sources too, but at a much lower intensity. So it is really the concentration of activity, rather than any single source, that makes the difference.

Q2. How does noise affect people's lives?
Constant noise seems to wear people down gradually rather than causing one dramatic effect. Poor sleep is probably the most common consequence, and that in turn affects concentration and mood at work or school. Some studies have linked long-term exposure to traffic noise with stress-related health problems, though I am not sure how conclusive that evidence is. At the very least, most people find it harder to relax when they cannot escape background noise.

Q3. Should governments do more to protect quiet spaces in cities?
I believe they should, within reasonable limits. Parks and pedestrian zones give residents somewhere to recover from the pressure of urban life, and the benefits for public health are fairly well documented. The difficulty is cost: land in city centres is extremely valuable, so setting it aside for green space means sacrificing housing or offices. A sensible balance might be to require new developments to include some public space rather than trying to redesign existing districts.

Q4. How might cities change in the future to become more peaceful?
Electric vehicles could make a noticeable difference, since engines and braking are a large share of street noise. Quieter road surfaces and stricter limits on night-time construction would also help, and some cities are already experimenting with these. I doubt cities will ever become truly quiet, though, because noise is almost a by-product of economic activity. The realistic goal is probably managing noise better rather than eliminating it.

2. Why it scores

  • TR (full response with developed ideas): Q3 takes a clear stance ("they should, within reasonable limits") and weighs benefits against costs instead of asserting. Band logic: position present throughout, ideas extended and supported. A memorised "both sides" paragraph that never commits would cap TR for an unclear position.
  • CC (paragraph logic, earned linkers): each answer builds in steps — Q1 narrows from multiple causes to one summary judgement ("So it is really the concentration of activity…"). Band logic: coherent sequencing, cohesive devices used flexibly. Chaining "Moreover / Furthermore" between unconnected points would cap CC for mechanical cohesion.
  • LR (natural less-common items): "at a much lower intensity", "wear people down gradually", "a by-product of economic activity" — precise, topic-fitted, not decorated. Hedging ("seems to", "probably", "might be") shows control of stance. A pre-learned environmental essay phrase forced in here would cap LR for poor collocation.
  • GRA (complex structures with accuracy): concessive and speculative range — "though I am not sure how conclusive that evidence is", "since engines and braking are a large share of street noise", "rather than trying to redesign existing districts". Clause control stays intact across 4–5 sentence answers. Template complex sentences that collapse mid-clause would count against GRA.

4. Grammar frames that carry ideas

  • Frame: speculative cause + hedge, "X seems to / is likely to + verb" — example: "Constant noise seems to wear people down gradually". Job: states an effect without claiming scientific certainty; essential for health and social questions.
  • Frame: realistic-limit closer, "The realistic goal is + -ing / noun" — example: "The realistic goal is probably managing noise better rather than eliminating it." Job: ends future-speculation answers with judgement instead of fantasy; reusable for environment and technology topics.

5. Outline to copy (not sentences)

  1. Q1: main cause (density) → second layer (commerce) → concession (countryside shares it) → summary judgement.
  2. Q2: overall characterisation (gradual) → most common effect → chain consequence → cautious research reference.
  3. Q3: stance with limit → benefit → cost/objection → compromise proposal.
  4. Q4: most promising change → supporting measures → doubt/limit → realistic goal.

6. 10-minute task + do not copy

Task: Answer a new question — "Should cars be banned from city centres?" — using the Q3 skeleton (stance with limit → benefit → cost → compromise). Speak for 40 seconds without using "Moreover" or "Furthermore".
Do not copy: "since engines and braking are a large share of street noise" — works only here because the topic is noise; reusing it on pollution or safety questions caps LR (inaccurate content) and TR (irrelevant detail).
