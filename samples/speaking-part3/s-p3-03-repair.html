S-P3-03 — REPAIR CULTURE & CONSUMERISM

Speaking Part 3 · Discussion · 4 questions · 3–5 sentences per answer · original prompt, not from any past paper

Paired Part 2 topic. Describe an object you repaired or tried to repair. You should say what the object was, what was wrong with it, what you did, and explain how you felt about the experience. Part 3 extension. The discussion below generalises from that single repair to consumer habits: why replacement beats repair, how generations differ, the environmental cost, and what policies could change behaviour.
Questions. 1. Why do people often replace things instead of repairing them? 2. Do older and younger generations have different attitudes to repair? 3. What are the environmental effects of constantly replacing products? 4. What could governments or companies do to encourage repair?

1. Sample answers (band 8+)

Q1. Why do people often replace things instead of repairing them?
In many cases it simply costs more to repair than to replace. A phone screen repair, for instance, can cost half the price of a new budget phone, and the repair may not last. Convenience plays a part too: ordering a replacement takes minutes, while finding a reliable repair shop takes effort. So for a lot of consumers, replacement feels like the rational choice, even if it is wasteful.

Q2. Do older and younger generations have different attitudes to repair?
There is some truth in the stereotype that older people repair more, but the reasons are practical rather than moral. They grew up when products were simpler, spare parts were available, and labour was cheaper relative to goods. Younger people, by contrast, face sealed devices and software-locked parts that are deliberately hard to fix at home. I would say the gap reflects what the market offers each generation more than any difference in values.

Q3. What are the environmental effects of constantly replacing products?
The most direct effect is probably the growth of electronic waste, much of which contains hazardous materials that are difficult to process safely. Manufacturing replacements also consumes raw materials and energy, so the damage happens twice: once in production and once in disposal. Some of this waste is recycled, but rates for small electronics remain low in most countries. Short product lifespans therefore multiply a problem that is already hard to manage.

Q4. What could governments or companies do to encourage repair?
The most effective step would likely be requiring spare parts and repair manuals to be available for a minimum number of years. Lower taxes on repair services could also narrow the price gap that currently pushes people toward replacement. Manufacturers might resist, since frequent replacement suits their business model, so regulation would need real enforcement. Still, a few countries have started "right to repair" rules, which suggests change is possible, if gradual.

2. Why it scores

  • TR (relevant, fully extended): Q2 handles a generalisation question responsibly — "the gap reflects what the market offers each generation" reframes rather than repeats the stereotype. Band logic: all parts answered, ideas developed with reasons. An unqualified "young people are wasteful" would cap TR for unsupported over-generalisation.
  • CC (clear progression, varied links): Q3 builds a chain — "the damage happens twice: once in production and once in disposal" — with the colon doing the cohesive work, not a memorised linker. Band logic: ideas sequenced logically within each answer. A memorised "Firstly / Moreover / In conclusion" frame around thin ideas would cap CC at 6–7.
  • LR (precise, flexible vocabulary): "spare parts were available", "software-locked parts", "hazardous materials", "narrow the price gap" — each collocates exactly with its subject. Hedging ("In many cases", "would likely be", "if gradual") is stance control, not filler. Imported "high-level" synonyms that break these pairings would cap LR.
  • GRA (range under control): contrast structures ("while finding a reliable repair shop takes effort"), cleft-like emphasis ("the damage happens twice"), and conditionals of concession ("since frequent replacement suits their business model, so regulation would need…"). Sentence length varies but grammar holds. Pre-memorised complex frames that misfire on new subjects would count against GRA.

4. Grammar frames that carry ideas

  • Frame: "They grew up when + past context" — example: "They grew up when products were simpler, spare parts were available…". Job: explains generational habits through conditions, not character; works for any "generations compared" question.
  • Frame: "The damage / problem happens twice: once in X and once in Y" — example: "the damage happens twice: once in production and once in disposal." Job: doubles the weight of an argument compactly; transfers to any cause-with-two-stages topic.

5. Outline to copy (not sentences)

  1. Q1: economic reason + example → convenience reason → summary of consumer logic.
  2. Q2: concede stereotype → explain with historical conditions → contrast young people's constraints → reframe the gap.
  3. Q3: most direct effect → upstream + downstream mechanism → recycling limitation → verdict on short lifespans.
  4. Q4: strongest measure → supporting measure → likely resistance → cautious precedent.
