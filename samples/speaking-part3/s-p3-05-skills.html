S-P3-05 — SKILLS & AMBITION

Speaking Part 3 · Discussion · 4 questions · 3–5 sentences per answer · original prompt, not from any past paper

Paired Part 2 topic. Describe a skill you would like to learn. You should say what the skill is, why you want to learn it, how you would learn it, and explain how it would help your future. Part 3 extension. The discussion below generalises from that personal ambition to the labour market: which skills matter now, how learning will change, and what employers value beyond degrees.
Questions. 1. Which skills are most valuable in today's job market? 2. How do you think learning new skills will change in the future? 3. Do employers care more about degrees or practical abilities? 4. Is ambition equally important in every career?

1. Sample answers (band 8+)

Q1. Which skills are most valuable in today's job market?
Digital literacy is probably near the top, since even non-technical roles now involve data, software and online communication. Communication itself remains highly valued, because teams are often spread across countries and time zones. Adaptability matters too: people who can retrain when their role changes have an obvious advantage. Technical knowledge gets you hired, but these broader abilities tend to decide how far you progress.

Q2. How do you think learning new skills will change in the future?
Learning will likely become shorter, more modular and more closely tied to specific jobs. Instead of multi-year courses, people may stack small certificates that each prove one competence. Artificial intelligence tutors could personalise this further by adjusting the pace and exercises to each learner. The risk is fragmentation: workers might collect narrow credentials without the deeper understanding a longer education provides.

Q3. Do employers care more about degrees or practical abilities?
It varies considerably by sector. In medicine, law and engineering, degrees remain a legal or professional requirement, so there is little debate. In creative and technical fields, however, portfolios and demonstrable output often carry more weight than certificates. Many large employers now use skills-based tests early in hiring, which suggests the balance is shifting. My sense is that degrees still open doors, but they no longer guarantee anything beyond the interview.

Q4. Is ambition equally important in every career?
Not entirely. In fast-moving fields like business or research, drive and willingness to compete are often essential for advancement. In caring professions such as nursing or teaching, though, patience and consistency arguably matter more than personal ambition. Too much ambition can even be harmful where teamwork and trust are central. So while motivation matters everywhere, the competitive form of ambition suits some careers far better than others.

2. Why it scores

  • TR (nuanced, complete answers): Q4 refuses the absolute — "the competitive form of ambition suits some careers far better than others" — distinguishing types of ambition rather than repeating the question. Band logic: ideas precise and fully developed. A memorised "success paragraph" about ambition in general would cap TR for drifting off the exact question.
  • CC (contrast and concession as glue): Q1 ends with a paired summary ("gets you hired… decide how far you progress") that binds the whole answer; Q3 moves sector by sector with "however" earned by a real turn. Band logic: progression clear, referencing flexible. Template chains of "Firstly / Moreover / In conclusion" would cap CC for mechanical cohesion.
  • LR (flexible, natural uncommon items): "digital literacy", "stack small certificates", "demonstrable output", "skills-based tests", "fragmentation" — each fits its sentence exactly. Speculation ("will likely", "could", "suggests") is graded, not vague. A pre-learned list of "advanced synonyms" inserted regardless of fit would cap LR for forced collocation.
  • GRA (varied complex grammar, accurate): concessive clauses ("since even non-technical roles now involve…", "where teamwork and trust are central"), future speculation with modals ("may stack", "could personalise"), and nominalisation ("willingness to compete", "demonstrable output"). Control is maintained throughout. Memorised frames that collapse when carrying new nouns would count against GRA.

4. Grammar frames that carry ideas

  • Frame: sector-splitter, "In X… In Y, however…" — example: "In medicine, law and engineering, degrees remain a requirement… In creative and technical fields, however, portfolios… often carry more weight". Job: answers "it depends" questions with concrete domains instead of vagueness; transfers to any sector-comparison topic.
  • Frame: future-risk closer, "The risk is + noun phrase" — example: "The risk is fragmentation: workers might collect narrow credentials without the deeper understanding…". Job: adds critical balance to optimistic speculation; the essential concession for "future" questions.

5. Outline to copy (not sentences)

  1. Q1: top skill + reason → second skill + reason → third skill → summary contrast (hired vs progress).
  2. Q2: prediction (shape of learning) → mechanism (certificates/AI) → risk → balanced verdict.
  3. Q3: qualify by sector → credential-led sectors → portfolio-led sectors → overall judgement.
  4. Q4: limit the absolute → where ambition helps → where other traits win → refined conclusion.

6. 10-minute task + do not copy

Task: Answer "Will university degrees become less important?" using the Q3 skeleton (sector split → credential-led → portfolio-led → overall judgement). Speak for 45 seconds, hedging at least twice.
Do not copy: "people may stack small certificates that each prove one competence" — works only here because the question is about future learning formats; lifting it into an unrelated answer caps LR (unnatural insertion) and CC (idea that leads nowhere).
