S-P3-04 — TRANSPORT & TRAFFIC

Speaking Part 3 · Discussion · 4 questions · 3–5 sentences per answer · original prompt, not from any past paper

Paired Part 2 topic. Describe a journey you made that involved heavy traffic. You should say where you were going, how you travelled, what the traffic was like, and explain how you felt about the experience. Part 3 extension. The discussion below moves from that one journey to transport systems: what causes congestion, public transport versus cars, and which solutions are worth their trade-offs.
Questions. 1. What are the main causes of traffic congestion in cities? 2. Is public transport a better option than private cars? 3. What solutions to congestion have been tried, and do they work? 4. How might patterns of commuting change in the future?

1. Sample answers (band 8+)

Q1. What are the main causes of traffic congestion in cities?
The fundamental cause is usually that road space has not kept up with the number of vehicles. As incomes rise, more households buy cars, but city centres cannot easily add new roads. Poorly coordinated traffic lights and parked cars narrowing busy streets make the existing capacity even less effective. Rush hour then concentrates all of this demand into the same two short windows each day.

Q2. Is public transport a better option than private cars?
For cities as a whole, public transport is generally more efficient, since one bus or train carries the passengers of dozens of cars. For individuals, though, cars win on flexibility, especially for families or people working unusual hours. Much also depends on quality: where trains are punctual and affordable, people switch voluntarily, but where they are overcrowded, few will give up their cars. So the better option varies with the system on offer, not just the principle.

Q3. What solutions to congestion have been tried, and do they work?
Congestion charges are probably the best-known measure, and cities like London and Singapore have seen real reductions in central traffic. The trade-off is fairness: the charge is a minor expense for wealthy drivers but a serious burden for low-income commuters. Expanding metro lines works more equitably, yet it requires enormous investment and years of disruptive construction. In practice, most cities end up combining several partial measures rather than finding one complete answer.

Q4. How might patterns of commuting change in the future?
Remote work has already flattened the traditional rush hour in some professions, and that trend may continue as companies accept hybrid arrangements. On the other hand, jobs in healthcare, construction and services cannot be done from home, so commuting will persist for a large share of workers. Cleaner buses and better cycling infrastructure could at least change how people commute, if not whether they do. Overall, I expect a more mixed pattern rather than the end of commuting.

2. Why it scores

  • TR (direct answers, weighed trade-offs): Q3 names a solution, judges it ("real reductions"), then states the fairness cost — both halves of "solutions + trade-offs" are covered. Band logic: all parts addressed with supported judgement. Listing measures without evaluating them would cap TR for under-development.
  • CC (contrast as structure): Q2 organises itself around "For cities as a whole… For individuals, though…" — the parallel contrast carries cohesion without any stacked linker. Band logic: ideas linked logically within sentences. Memorised "On the one hand / On the other hand" scaffolding around thin content would cap CC for mechanical signalling.
  • LR (topic-precise vocabulary): "road space has not kept up with", "rush hour", "congestion charges", "hybrid arrangements", "cycling infrastructure" — each is the natural term for its idea. Hedged scope ("generally", "in some professions", "a large share") prevents over-claiming. Generic "fancy" synonyms swapped into these slots would cap LR for weak collocation.
  • GRA (complex sentences, clean control): concessive clauses ("where trains are punctual…", "while…"), result structures ("make the existing capacity even less effective"), and future speculation ("may continue", "I expect a more mixed pattern"). Range serves the argument throughout. Copied complex frames that break under new content would count against GRA.

4. Grammar frames that carry ideas

  • Frame: double-perspective contrast, "For X as a whole… For individuals, though…" — example: "For cities as a whole, public transport is generally more efficient… For individuals, though, cars win on flexibility". Job: answers "which is better" questions by splitting the viewpoint; reusable for any policy dilemma.
  • Frame: trade-off sentence, "The trade-off is + noun phrase" — example: "The trade-off is fairness: the charge is a minor expense for wealthy drivers but…". Job: names the cost of a solution in one clause; works for environment, technology, and urban topics.

5. Outline to copy (not sentences)

  1. Q1: root cause (supply vs demand) → aggravating factors → timing factor (rush hour).
  2. Q2: systemic verdict → individual counterpoint → conditional factor (quality) → synthesis.
  3. Q3: measure + evidence → fairness cost → alternative + its cost → realistic conclusion.
  4. Q4: existing trend → limit (jobs that cannot move) → possible modal shift → overall expectation.

6. 10-minute task + do not copy

Task: Answer "Should cycling be encouraged in large cities?" with the Q2 skeleton (systemic verdict → individual counterpoint → conditional factor → synthesis). Four sentences, no "Moreover" or "Furthermore".
Do not copy: "cities like London and Singapore have seen real reductions in central traffic" — works only here because congestion charging is the question; citing it elsewhere caps TR (unprompted example) and risks LR penalty if the facts do not fit.
