S-P2-01 — A PERSON WHO TAUGHT YOU SOMETHING USEFUL

Speaking Part 2 · Person cue card · ~210 words · ~1.5–2 min · original prompt, not from any past paper

Prompt. Describe a person who taught you something useful. You should say: who this person is — what they taught you — how they taught you — and explain why what you learned mattered to you. (Original cue card; you have one minute to prepare and should speak for 1–2 minutes.)

1. Sample answer (band 8+)

I would like to talk about my uncle, who taught me how to cook a proper family meal when I was about sixteen. He had been a chef for years, and during one summer holiday he offered to show me the basics, since I could barely boil rice at the time.

At first he just made me watch how he prepared everything before lighting the stove — chopping vegetables evenly, seasoning in small amounts, tasting as he went. Then he handed me the knife and stood beside me, correcting my grip and showing me how to keep a steady rhythm instead of rushing. I burned the first dish completely, and instead of getting annoyed he laughed and explained what the smell of burning garlic actually tells you about the heat. By the third week I could manage a simple three-dish dinner on my own, which felt like a genuine achievement.

What mattered most was not the recipes but the patience behind them. He kept saying that cooking is really about paying attention to other people — noticing what they like and adjusting. That summer changed how I think about learning anything difficult. I still hear his voice whenever I am in the kitchen, reminding me to slow down and taste before serving.

2. Why it scores

  • TR: Covers all four bullets with nothing skipped: the person ("my uncle… had been a chef"), the skill ("how to cook a proper family meal"), the method ("correcting my grip", "explained what the smell… tells you"), and the reflection ("changed how I think about learning anything difficult"). Band 8 logic: all parts sufficiently addressed with extended, supported ideas. A memorised person-description (appearance/personality list) that ignored the "how they taught you" bullet would cap TR.
  • CC: Time sequencing is logical without heavy signposting: "At first… Then… By the third week… What mattered most…". The opener orients the listener, the middle holds two clear story beats, and the final paragraph reflects. Band 8 logic: ideas sequenced logically, cohesion managed well. Chained fillers ("Moreover, furthermore, in conclusion") would make cohesion mechanical and cap CC at 6–7.
  • LR: Precise, topic-fitted verbs and collocations: "correcting my grip", "keep a steady rhythm", "seasoning in small amounts", "a genuine achievement". Nothing is decorative; every uncommon item fits cooking and learning. Band 8 logic: fluent, flexible range with skilful uncommon use. Dropping in a memorised "fancy" phrase that does not collocate with cooking would cap LR.
  • GRA: Past tenses are controlled and varied: past simple ("he handed me the knife"), past perfect ("He had been a chef"), past continuous implied in narration, plus present reflection ("cooking is really about…"). The majority of sentences are error-free. Band 8 logic: wide range with only occasional slips. A memorised complex frame forced onto new content typically breaks tense consistency and caps GRA.

4. Grammar frames that carry ideas

  • Frame: Past perfect background + past simple event ("He had been a chef for years, and… he offered to show me") — example: "He had been a chef for years". Job: sets up the teacher's credibility before the story starts; reuse for any person cue.
  • Frame: "What mattered most was not X but Y" — example: "What mattered most was not the recipes but the patience behind them." Job: delivers the closing reflection with weight; reuse to answer any "explain why" bullet.

5. Outline to copy (not sentences)

  1. Orient: name the person + relationship + what + when (one sentence covering who/what).
  2. Beat 1: how they taught — observed detail, then guided practice.
  3. Beat 2: a failure or turning point + what they said or did.
  4. Reflect: why it mattered — the larger lesson, carried into the present.

6. 10-minute task + do not copy

Task: Retell this monologue in 90 seconds from the outline only, swapping the skill to something you really learned (cycling, an instrument, a work skill). Keep the "failure + what they said" beat and the "not X but Y" closing frame.
Do not copy: "he laughed and explained what the smell of burning garlic actually tells you about the heat" — works only here because it is the sensory proof of this exact cooking lesson; reuse the move (expert reads a small signal), not the sentence.
